# E20: does winner-chasing inflate the naive test when the scoring is not additive?

Committed before running. Code: `experiments/e20_non_additive.py`,
`searchers/crossover.py`.

## Question

Every result so far (P4, P5, E15–E19) lives in a world where a specification
is a sum of base columns. That is what lets the recursive bootstrap
reconstruct candidates the search never evaluated, and what lets
`full_class_matrix` enumerate a class. Moving-average crossover rules are not
like that: a rule's position is the sign of a difference of two moving
averages, so its return column is not a linear combination of other rules'
columns.

Three things are tested in that world:

- **(a)** Does a search that builds on its own winner still inflate the naive
  realized-menu Reality Check? P4's lemma does not cover this setting, so the
  direction is predicted without a theoretical guarantee.
- **(b)** Does the mirror hold — does anchoring on the *loser* leave the naive
  test at or below nominal?
- **(c)** Do the gate's two valid paths work here: the procedure-level
  bootstrap (valid for any specification class) and a declared explicit class
  (P3), which needs no reconstruction.

## World

- Daily log returns of a **driftless random walk**, so no rule has an edge and
  a rejection is a type-I error by construction. With drift, long-biased rules
  would earn a positive Sharpe under the shift null.
- **T = 5040 periods** after a **burn-in of 200**, which equals the slowest
  moving average in the grid. Any grid with a slower window needs a longer
  burn-in.
- **Grid (declared before the search):** fast ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12,
  15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50}, slow ∈ {10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 60, 75, 100,
  125, 150, 175, 200}, long-only ∈ {false, true}, keeping fast < slow: **362
  rules per asset**.
- **Two worlds, n = 2000 draws each:** a single price path, and a panel of 5
  independent paths (1,810 rules). Measured effective breadth is about 3.3 and
  16.5. The single-asset world is the one the bundled examples live in; the
  panel exists because breadth near 3 damps the effect under test, so a null
  result there would speak to correlation rather than to non-additivity.

## Searchers

`searchers/crossover.py`. Both evaluate a coarse sub-grid, take an anchor from
those results, then refine by evaluating the anchor's neighbours on the grid,
twice. Both submit the best rule they evaluated; only the anchor rule differs.

- **winner-anchored:** the anchor is the best coarse result.
- **loser-anchored:** the anchor is the worst coarse result.

**Class closure is enforced.** Refinement steps move between adjacent indices
of the declared fast and slow lists, so every rule a search can reach is on
the grid. A proposal off the grid raises, and a test covers it.

## Nulls

Five per draw, all on the same simulated path.

| null | construction | used for |
|---|---|---|
| naive (winner) | Reality Check on the rules the winner-anchored search logged | (a) |
| naive (loser) | Reality Check on the rules the loser-anchored search logged | (b) |
| procedure-level | re-execute the search on each shifted surrogate | (c), gold standard |
| explicit class, shifts | class maximum on the **same shifts** | (c), exact P3 check |
| explicit class, bootstrap | Reality Check over the whole class | (c), the gate's own path |

- **Shifts.** Positions come from the real price path, which nullification
  leaves untouched, and are paired with circularly shifted forward returns.
  Shifts are drawn **uniformly without replacement** from
  `[200, T − 200]`, a pool of 4,641; the excluded window is 200, the slowest
  moving average, because shifts near zero or T nearly preserve the real
  alignment. `rerun_B = 500`.
- **Effective independence, measured before running.** Adjacent shifts give
  nearly identical surrogates: lag-1 autocorrelation 0.90, autocorrelation
  time ≈ 42, so the shift null carries about **110 effectively independent
  draws** (median over 8 seeds, range 32–144), however many are drawn.
  Resolution near the 5% tail is governed by that number, not by
  1/(rerun_B + 1). Evenly spaced shifts were tested and are worse than random
  draws (critical-value sampling sd 0.0156 against 0.0089), which is why
  random sampling is used.

  `rerun_B` is 500 rather than 1000 for the same reason: with ~110 effective
  draws, shifts beyond about 500 add little. Measured, the critical value's
  sampling sd is 0.0134 at 500 against 0.0089 at 1000, on a critical value of
  roughly 0.6. The saving is large (the shift arms dominate the run), but the
  reason for the choice is the measured dependence, not the cost.
- **Engine.** All bootstrap nulls use `garden._engine.null_max_bootstrap`,
  which reproduces the estimator's null to 3×10⁻¹⁴ and is 2–39× faster on
  these shapes. B = 1500.

## Decision rules

Thresholds at n = 2000: the one-sided exact binomial against 5% rejects at
**117/2000 (5.85%)** and has 80% power at a true rate of **6.3%**.

1. **Procedure-level is nominal (gold standard).** Its type-I rate at α = 0.05
   is not significantly above 5%: Wilson lower bound at most 5%. If this
   fails, nothing else in the experiment is interpreted.
2. **Explicit class under the same shifts is conservative (exact P3 check).**
   Its p-value is at least the procedure-level p-value on every draw. Both
   sides are computed **with the same engine**, and only violations larger
   than **10⁻¹⁰** are counted, because the rerun's own pick is itself a column
   of the class, so an exact tie decided by floating-point reassociation is
   not a P3 violation. This is E18's lesson one level down.
3. **The gate's own path is valid and agrees.** The explicit-class bootstrap
   Reality Check has a Wilson interval compatible with 5% or below, and its
   rejection decisions agree with rule 2's shift-based version on at least
   **99%** of draws.
4. **Naive, winner-anchored: predicted inflated.** One-sided binomial against
   5%. Exactly one of three outcomes, fixed now:
   - **inflated:** p < 0.05;
   - **not detectably inflated:** p ≥ 0.05, meaning no detectable inflation
     for this class at this breadth, which would not refute P4 in additive
     settings;
   - **conservative:** significantly below 5% by the two-sided test.
   There is no theoretical guarantee here: P4's lemma assumes a symmetric,
   nondecreasing pair statistic, which crossover scoring does not provide.
5. **Naive, loser-anchored: predicted at or below nominal.** Same three
   outcomes, same lack of guarantee. A reversal between rules 4 and 5 in this
   world would support the sign result outside additive scoring.

## Secondary, reported but not deciding

1. Per-world effective breadth, and the block lengths `select_block_length`
   chose (measured beforehand: 1 or 2 on 159 of 160 draws).
2. The draw-by-draw ordering of all five p-values, descriptively.
3. The size and count of rule-2 violations below the 10⁻¹⁰ tolerance.
4. Naive winner against naive loser, paired by draw (exact McNemar).
5. The share of draws where the explicit-class bootstrap and shift versions
   disagree, and in which direction.

## Cost

Measured end to end, one draw at a time: **45.2 CPU-seconds** in the
single-asset world and **66.2** on the panel. At n = 2000 per world that is
about **62 CPU-hours**, roughly 2 hours on 28 workers.

The estimate has moved twice, both times before anything ran, and the history
matters for reading the cost of future experiments here:

- A first estimate of 49 CPU-hours came from component timings (a shift
  surrogate is 3.43 ms at one asset and 16.91 ms on the panel; the chunked
  bootstrap at B = 1500 costs about 0.28 s and 1.86 s at the block lengths
  `select_block_length` actually returns, which were 1 or 2 on 159 of 160
  draws). Measured end to end it was **102 CPU-hours**: each shift does more
  than the surrogate multiply, since it also computes a Sharpe for every class
  column and runs both searches.
- Two changes then brought it to 62. `rerun_B` went from 1000 to 500 for the
  reason given under Shifts, and the searches now read the class Sharpe vector
  the shift loop already computes instead of recomputing their own columns.
  The second change also strengthens rule 2: both sides of the inequality come
  from one vector. The panel gained most (129.1 → 66.2 CPU-s per draw) because
  its searches were recomputing about 230 columns per shift; the single-asset
  world gained less (54.8 → 45.2), since the bootstrap arms are a fixed cost
  that neither change touches.

n stays at 2000 because the 6.3% minimum detectable rate is what makes the
naive arm worth running at all, and both worlds stay because the panel is the
only one where the effect is not damped by correlation to invisibility.
