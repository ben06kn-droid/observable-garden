# The Observable Garden

*Fixed and adaptive candidate sets under the DSR* · Ben Knaster

## 1. Abstract

A backtest is usually the best of many attempts, so its Sharpe ratio must be judged against what the best of many attempts would look like on pure noise. The deflated Sharpe ratio (DSR) does this. A question remains when the search is adaptive, meaning that what the researcher tries next depends on what has already been seen.

“Under adaptive specification search, a DSR null that conditions only on the realized candidate set can be anti-conservative unless the adaptive search mechanism is incorporated into the null-generating process” (López de Prado, correspondence, 2026). This note makes that statement precise and answers three questions about the error.

- **Direction.** It depends on what the search builds on. A search that builds on its current best strategy makes the test too lenient. One that builds on its current worst makes it too strict. One that builds on a strategy chosen at random leaves it exactly right.
- **Size.** In a simple two-stage search, the error is set by the number of features, how correlated they are, and how many rounds the search runs. It has an explicit formula, and pre-registered simulations match it.
- **Remedy.** The error is repaired by keeping the right record of the search. There are three levels of record, and the strongest has been built as a working system, called Don Quixote.

That a test becomes unreliable as the number of candidates grows is well known (Freedman 1983; Dwork et al. 2015). What is measured here is how fast, in which direction, and what record fixes it.

## 2. Setup

**Objects.** There are K base return series, called features, observed over T periods. A trial is one computation of a Sharpe ratio (López de Prado & Porcu 2026, fn. 1). A search is a rule that looks at the data and produces two things: the candidate set, which is every strategy it evaluated, and one selected strategy with its reported Sharpe ratio. In an adaptive search the candidate set itself depends on the data. K counts features, not trials; the number of trials is the size of the candidate set.

**Null.** Every feature has a true mean of zero, so any apparent performance is luck. With enough data, the Sharpe ratios of the candidates are jointly normal, with correlations inherited from the features (Ledoit & Wolf 2008). Most results below are proved for a large sample and for exchangeable features: equal volatilities and one common correlation ω.

López de Prado & Porcu judge the reported strategy against “the null distribution of the maximum generated by the strategy selection process”. This can mean two things.

- **The process null:** re-run the whole search on noise, including its choice of what to try, and record the best result.
- **The realized-set null:** keep the list of candidates the search actually produced, and record the best of that fixed list on noise.

The two agree when the candidate list does not depend on the data. When it does, they can differ. This note measures the difference.

**Estimation.** Both nulls are estimated with the stationary bootstrap (Politis & Romano 1994). It reshuffles the history in blocks, using the same blocks for every feature, so correlation across features and over time is preserved. Each feature is first shifted to mean zero, which imposes the null (White 2000). The realized-set version reshuffles the logged strategies and keeps the list frozen. The process version re-runs the search on every reshuffle.

The tests ask whether the best result of a search is distinguishable from nothing. They do not ask whether one particular strategy is good given that it was selected.

## 3. The fixed candidate set

**Proposition 1 (known: White 2000; Hansen 2005).** If the candidate list is chosen without looking at the returns, the realized-set test has the correct error rate:

$$
P(p \le \alpha) \le \alpha + o(1),
$$

with equality in the limit when every true mean is zero. The list may be fixed in advance or drawn by a rule whose randomness is unrelated to the returns. Ledoit & Wolf (2008) extend the result from means to Sharpe ratios. The trials may be as correlated as one likes. What matters is that the list was not chosen from the data.

**Proposition 2 (known).** If a search reports a value no larger than the maximum of a set whose null is valid, the test stays valid. It loses power but never validity, because a lower reported value can only raise the p-value. This is what later lets a class declared in advance cover any search inside it (Section 6).

Both were checked by simulation. Scripted searches with fixed candidate lists, from one trial to 1,500 correlated trials, produced p-values that could not be told apart from uniform. A greedy rule applied to a list written out in advance, and tested against the whole list, also showed no detectable excess. The same greedy rule did show an excess when it generated its own candidates. The condition that matters is not independence between trials. It is that the list ignores the data.

## 4. Adaptive search

**How the error arises.** Take a two-stage search. It evaluates all K single features, takes the winner as its anchor, then evaluates every pair that contains the anchor. The realized-set bootstrap reshuffles the data but keeps the logged pairs, so every reshuffle still builds its pairs around the feature that won on the real data. On fresh noise a different feature would usually win. The real search chose its anchor from the data; no reshuffle does. The frozen version therefore explores less than the search it stands in for, and its p-values come out too small.

**Lemma.** Order the single-feature scores as z(1) ≥ … ≥ z(K), and let g be a pair score that is symmetric and increasing in each argument. Then for every feature a:

$$
g\big(z_{(1)}, z_{(K)}\big) \;\le\; \max_{j \ne a} g\big(z_a, z_j\big) \;\le\; g\big(z_{(1)}, z_{(2)}\big)
$$

The best pair containing any fixed feature is never better than the best pair containing the winner, and never worse than the best pair containing the loser. The lemma needs one common pair score. That holds in the large-sample limit with exchangeable features, where an equal-weight pair scores

$$
g(z_i, z_j) = \frac{z_i + z_j}{\sqrt{2 + 2\omega}}
$$

**Direction.** On every reshuffle, the winner-anchored search reaches a maximum at least as high as the frozen list does. The realized-set p-value is therefore never larger than the process p-value, and the test is too lenient. Anchoring on the loser reverses every inequality, and the test is too strict.

**The rank mixture.** Freeze the anchor at the feature that won on the real data. On a reshuffle of exchangeable noise, that feature is equally likely to rank anywhere from 1 to K. So the realized-set null is the plain average of K process nulls, one for each rank the search could anchor on:

$$
F_C(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} F_P^{(k)}(x)
$$

Here F\_C is the realized-set null and F\_P^(k) is the process null of a search that anchors on its rank-k feature. Freezing the anchor amounts to anchoring on a rank drawn at random.

Two consequences follow.

1. A search that anchors on a feature chosen uniformly at random is exactly calibrated under the realized-set test. Such an anchor ignores the data, so this is Proposition 1.
2. Averaged over the K ranks, the error rates of the realized-set test come to exactly the nominal level. Anchoring does not create error. It moves error between ranks: the top two ranks are too lenient, the bottom rank is too strict, and the ranks in between are settled by calculation.

**Evidence.** Each prediction was registered before its simulation ran.

- The realized-set test rejected too often when the search anchored on its winner, and at or below its nominal rate when it anchored on a random feature or on its worst. The process bootstrap returned every case to nominal.
- When the anchor was drawn with a probability that leaned gradually from the worst feature toward the best, the error rate rose steadily and crossed the nominal level where the draw was uniform. What matters is not adaptivity as such but how strongly the next step is tied to past performance.
- With the anchor fixed at a given rank, the measured error rates matched the limit values computed in advance.
- With finite samples and with unequal correlations, where the lemma is not proved, the predicted ordering still held on almost every reshuffle.
- For strategies that are not weighted sums of features (moving-average crossover rules), the direction of the asymmetry appeared but the error was small. No size is claimed there.

The two-stage search is not the general adaptive case. It is the case in which the direction can be proved and the size computed. For a search that adapts at every step, with restarts and a rule for when to stop, validity has to come from the record of the search (Section 6).

## 5. Size

With exchangeable features the realized-set null is the same rank mixture whichever feature was frozen, so it does not depend on the data. The limiting error rate of the realized-set test at level α is the chance that the process maximum clears the realized-set critical value:

$$
\text{error rate} \;=\; 1 - F_P\big(F_C^{-1}(1-\alpha)\big) \;=\; \alpha + \big[F_C(c) - F_P(c)\big], \qquad c = F_C^{-1}(1-\alpha)
$$

For the two-stage search under winner-anchoring, the process null can be written as an integral over the common factor f shared by the features:

$$
F_P(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \varphi(f)\, G_K\big(A(f), B(f)\big)\, df, \qquad
G_K(A, B) = K \int_{-\infty}^{A} \varphi(u)\, \Phi\big(\min(u,\, B-u)\big)^{K-1}\, du
$$

$$
A(f) = \frac{x - \sqrt{\omega}\, f}{\sqrt{1-\omega}}, \qquad
B(f) = \frac{\sqrt{2+2\omega}\; x - 2\sqrt{\omega}\, f}{\sqrt{1-\omega}}
$$

When the features are uncorrelated (ω = 0), the outer integral drops out, with A = x and B = √2·x. The realized-set null follows from the rank mixture of Section 4.

Three things drive the size.

- **More features, more error.** With 80 uncorrelated features, roughly a third of searches run on pure noise pass a test meant to pass one in twenty.
- **Correlation.** When features are correlated, the best pair beats the best single feature less often, so freezing the anchor gives up less. Correlation among trials is not the cause of this error. It is what limits it.
- **Depth.** A third round, building triples around the winning pair, raises the error further.

Simulations registered in advance matched the limit values in every cell, and the growth with the number of features was statistically clear. One further prediction was not confirmed: with a random anchor in round two and re-anchoring in round three, the excess was too small for the sample to detect.

## 6. Don Quixote

In correspondence, López de Prado noted that a transcript could be sufficient if, together with the code, data, random seeds and decision rules, it lets the search be reconstructed and re-run under the null. The results above say what each kind of record buys.

| What the log keeps | What it supports | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Returns of the strategies tried | The realized-set test, valid only if the candidate list did not depend on the data | None, but wrong under adaptive search |
| Returns, plus a class of strategies declared before the search | A valid test for any search inside the class | Conservative: power is lost |
| A set of rules that can be re-run | The process null itself, under adaptive search | The search must be run through a harness |

**The second level** is the approach of Sullivan, Timmermann & White (1999). The class is fixed before the search, so Proposition 1 gives its maximum a valid null. The search reports at most that maximum, so Proposition 2 applies. Here the class is every signed combination of up to three of 40 features, 82,240 strategies in all. Its cost was measured. A searcher that submits the best strategy in the class is tested at close to the nominal rate. A greedy search inside the class is tested well below it, because it can reach only a small part of the class and is still charged for all of it.

**The third level is Don Quixote,** a harness for AI agents. López de Prado & Porcu recommend simulating the actual model-selection pipeline under adaptive selection. Don Quixote makes that simulation re-runnable from a log. Its design rests on one fact: the searcher names a move, and the harness carries it out. The searcher cannot submit a strategy of its own, so the log cannot disagree with what ran.

- **Next** is a move from a short fixed list: add one feature to the best strategy so far, swap one feature for another, or flip a sign. Rejected proposals are logged too.
- **Stop/Restart** must be a declared condition on what the searcher has seen, such as the best result clearing a bar or several failures in a row. The value it saw is recorded before the decision takes effect.

On each reshuffle the harness re-runs the logged moves and re-checks every stopping condition. A reshuffle may therefore extend a different feature or stop at a different time, as its own data dictate. Before any test is run, the harness replays the original data and requires the original sequence back, action for action. A log that fails this is refused. In testing, replay was exact: scripted searchers were reproduced bit for bit.

**Stopping is the same error, in time.** What a searcher tries next is a rule and replays exactly. When it stops was decided after seeing results. Freezing those decisions repeats the realized-set error: under a stop-when-cleared rule, a run that reports a high value stopped early, so its frozen reshuffles search less than the rule would have. This was measured, and freezing the stopping decisions did raise the error rate. Declaring the stopping rule, and re-checking it on every reshuffle, brought the rate back to nominal or below. Rates below nominal are what Proposition 2 predicts for a searcher that submits less than its maximum.

**Beyond the null.** A test must also behave where an edge exists, and on real features. Scripted searchers were run on real ETF features with synthetic returns and a planted edge of graded size. None of the registered validity tests failed, and submissions that passed outperformed those that did not on data held back from the search. The bootstrap’s estimates of the class maximum were accurate on these features, and the lower confidence bounds on the selected Sharpe ratio covered at their stated rates.

For an agent, the second level comes by construction, because its tools fix the class before it searches. The third is available when its decisions are typed moves the harness executes and its stopping rules are declared. What an agent considers without evaluating never enters the log, so every logged count is a floor (López de Prado & Fabozzi 2026 describe the human analogue).

## 7. Limitations

- **The direction is a limit result.** It is proved for large samples and one common pair score, which requires equal volatilities and correlations. With finite samples it is measured, not proved.
- **Other scoring rules.** Outside weighted sums of features the asymmetry is measured, but no size is established and nothing is proved.
- **The process bootstrap is only partly justified in theory.** It is covered for rules that depend on the ranking of Sharpe ratios (van der Vaart 1998; Politis & Romano 1994). For correlation-based anchoring with equal correlations the argument is a sketch, and with unequal correlations it is open.
- **The number of trials is held fixed.** Proposition 1 assumes it stays fixed as the sample grows. The 1,500-trial grid and the 82,240-strategy class are supported by simulation, not by the theorem.
- **Untested structures.** Block correlation and unequal volatilities have not been tested.
- **Stopping-rule replay.** Its error rates are at or below nominal on the searchers tested. Exactness is not claimed and nothing is proved.
- **Scope of the evidence.** Sections 3 to 6 concern scripted searchers and synthetic returns. Whether a result that passes holds out of sample on real data is untested.
- **What is never logged.** Whatever a searcher considers without evaluating is invisible to every level of record.

## 8. Closing

Under adaptive search, a DSR null that conditions only on the realized candidate set errs in a direction set by what the search builds on, by an amount set by the number of features, their correlation and the depth of the search. A record that lets the search be re-run repairs it, and that record has been built.

The record does not supply power. The harness has since been run with language-model agents (Claude Sonnet 5). On synthetic panels with a planted edge, the agents usually earned a pass where an edge existed and never where none did. On a panel of real ETFs, no submission passed, thus the current work is on power.

The first step is ridge\_stack, a fixed prediction model that combines ridge regressions with small gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM). It lets the harness evaluate non-linear strategies that the class of simple feature combinations cannot express. Because the model is fixed before it sees the data, its output counts as one strategy declared in advance, which is the second level of record with a class of one. In a registered test on synthetic panels it kept the nominal error rate and passed planted non-linear edges far more often than the class did. On real data, one panel of industry portfolios so far, it has not yet produced a pass.

**Reproduction.** The limit values are computed two ways, by simulation and by numerical integration of the formula in Section 5, and the two agree to three decimal places. Each measured value comes from a named experiment. The experiments of Sections 4 to 6 were registered before they ran, at commits listed in the ledger. Code, registrations and data are at github.com/ben06kn-droid/observable-garden. The propositions are numbered differently in the repository’s theory file, and a table there maps one to the other.

**Related work.** White (2000), Hansen (2005) and Romano & Wolf (2005) treat a fixed candidate set. Dwork et al. (2015), Blum & Hardt (2015) and Hardt (2017) bound the error of adaptive estimation in general, without a direction tied to how the search anchors.
